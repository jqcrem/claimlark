The problem

Inquiries arrive as stories, not as matters.

People explain what happened the way anyone would: out of order, with the details a firm needs mixed in among the ones it doesn’t.

  • The basics take several conversations.

    Dates, parties, and jurisdiction often surface only after a few rounds of calls and emails.

  • Every intake note looks different.

    What gets written down depends on who took the call, so comparing or handing off an inquiry means redoing work.

  • Inquiries outside your practice still take time.

    Each one has to be read, answered, and redirected, and the firm that fits rarely receives it already organized.

How it works

From a first account to a matter your firm can act on.

  1. Received

    The account comes in

    A prospective client describes what happened in their own words. ClaimLark keeps that account as written.

  2. Structured

    A matter summary is drafted

    Matter type, jurisdiction, dates, parties, key facts, and available documents go into one consistent format.

  3. Needs review

    Your team reviews it

    Missing information is flagged and conflict information is collected for firm review. A person at the firm decides what happens next.

  4. Ready to route

    It goes where it fits

    A reviewed matter is ready for your intake team, or, through the network, for a participating firm whose practice areas fit.

Two halves

Better intake for your firm. Better-fit inquiries from the network.

Referral network

Participating firms set their practice areas and receive inquiries that fit them, already structured. A public intake assistant, which will bring inquiries into the network, is in development.

Benefits and boundaries

Organized intake. Decisions stay with people.

What it’s for

  • Less back-and-forth to collect the basics of a matter.
  • One consistent summary format, whoever took the inquiry.
  • Gaps listed up front, so the first call starts from what’s missing.
  • Inquiries matched to your practice areas through the network.

What it doesn’t do

  • Give legal advice or assess the merits of a matter.
  • Accept clients or create an attorney–client relationship.
  • Make decisions. A person at the firm reviews every summary.
  • Guarantee referrals, case volume, or outcomes.
